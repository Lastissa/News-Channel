1. The publishing workflow

Every story follows the same path. Skipping a step is what usually causes a rejected publish or a correction later.

  1. Open the editor. Add news lives in the header for every staff account.
  2. Write a headline you can defend. It is limited to 100 characters, it must say what actually happened, and you cannot change it later.
  3. Add a banner. Paste an image link, upload a file, or pick one from the archive. Add an image description so the picture is accessible and explained.
  4. Write the body. Lead with the facts: who, what, where and when. Then add detail in short sections with headings.
  5. Pick a category. It decides where the story is listed and which similar stories readers are offered.
  6. Review before you publish. The editor runs the checks below and blocks a story that cannot go out. Warnings you can read and confirm.
  7. Publish. The story goes live immediately and is announced to newsletter readers.

2. Do's and don'ts

If you remember nothing else, remember these.

Do

  • Check every fact, name, date and figure before you publish.
  • Open with new information. The first paragraph should not repeat the headline.
  • Say each fact once, then move on. Repetition hurts trust and reach.
  • Use short sections with real headings so readers can scan.
  • Give the banner a clear description. It is shown beneath the picture.
  • Credit the source when a story is based on another outlet's reporting.
  • Write for people first, then for search engines.

Don't

  • Do not publish a fact you have not confirmed.
  • Do not copy another writer's work. Plagiarism is a removal offence.
  • Do not start the body with a heading that repeats the headline.
  • Do not separate sections with lines of symbols such as ------ or =====. Use a blank line or a real heading.
  • Do not leave empty headings such as a lone #.
  • Do not pad a story with filler. A very short story is a warning, a padded one reads as noise.
  • Do not pass off an AI generated image as a real news photograph.

3. Formatting codes

The codes are optional, but they are what turn a wall of text into a readable story. Everything below is typed on its own line.

Captions: the description you type after an image URL becomes that image's caption. Without one, no caption is shown.

4. What the review checks

The editor inspects the story as readers will see it. A blocked story will not publish. A warning asks you to confirm before it goes out.

CheckResult
No body text, or a body that is only a headingBlocked
An empty heading, such as a lone #Blocked
A heading that is only symbolsBlocked
A decorative separator line such as ------Blocked
A long run of repeated symbolsBlocked
Filler content with no real informationBlocked
The body opens with a heading that repeats the headlineBlocked at a very close match, warned from a strong match
The first paragraph repeats the headlineBlocked at a very close match, warned from a strong match
A body under 40 wordsWarning, you can confirm it
The body starts with any heading at allWarning, a normal opening paragraph reads better

Heads up: "blocked at a very close match" means 90% or more similar, and "warned from a strong match" means from 75%. The full text of the finding is shown in the editor, so read it before you change anything.

5. Pictures and captions

  • A banner can be a pasted image link, an uploaded file, or a picture from the archive.
  • An uploaded file gets a quality setting. Low loads fastest, high shows the most detail and is the heaviest.
  • The image description (image_info) is shown under the picture. Write what the picture shows, not a copy of the headline.
  • Replacing an uploaded banner overwrites the same stored file, so a swapped picture does not leave a duplicate behind.
  • Pasted links stay exactly as you typed them. The site does not take ownership of, or delete, an image that only lives on someone else's server.

6. Editing a published story

You can edit your own stories from your profile. Some edits carry more weight than others.

  • The headline is locked. Changing it after publishing would break the story's link, so it is not editable. Get it right the first time.
  • A category or body change is a real update. It refreshes the "last updated" notice readers see and the date search engines are told about.
  • A picture or caption change is cosmetic. It updates the banner on the page but leaves the "last updated" date exactly where it was.
  • Switching from an uploaded picture to a pasted link releases the old uploaded file, unless another story still shares it.

7. Deleting a story

Deleting a story removes it from the site for good. Do it from your profile's published list, and treat it as final.

  • The story page, its comments and its bookmarks go with it.
  • An uploaded banner is released from storage at the same time, so deleting a story no longer leaves its picture behind forever.
  • A pasted external link is never touched. The file was never ours to delete.
  • If another story still shares the same uploaded picture, the picture is kept so the surviving story does not lose its banner.
  • Deleting your whole account cleans up every story banner the same way.

8. Reusing pictures from the archive

  • The archive holds pictures and files that are cleared and described, ready to drop into any story. Use Browse archive in the editor.
  • An archived picture can be inserted left, right or centred with imgl, imgr or imgc, or as a download with filel.
  • Reuse is encouraged. A good, relevant picture is better than a borrowed screenshot that might disappear.
  • Give the picture a description that fits your story, rather than reusing the old caption word for word.

9. Using AI

AI is allowed. Publishing something you have not checked is not. The rule is simple: the tool can help you write, but the name on the story is yours, and so is the responsibility for every word in it.

What AI is good for

  • Brainstorming angles and headline options.
  • Turning rough notes into a clean first draft.
  • Tightening a paragraph or cutting repetition.
  • Translating, or explaining a technical term in plain language.
  • Summarising a long document you have already read and understood.

What you must never do

  • Publish a fact, figure, date, name, quote or link you have not confirmed against a primary source. AI invents all of these with total confidence.
  • Publish without reading every line and checking it against the source material.
  • Present an AI generated image as a real photograph of a real event.
  • Paste private, personal or embargoed material into a third party AI tool.
  • Use AI to rewrite another outlet's story and pass it off as original reporting.

Writing a full report with AI

Writing a whole story with AI is allowed, under three conditions. It is a draft for you to work from, not a finished article.

  1. Verify every fact independently. If AI says a meeting happened on a date, confirm that date against a real source. Remove anything you cannot confirm.
  2. Edit it into our voice. Rewrite the parts that read like a machine. Make sure the story leads with what actually happened.
  3. Tell the desk. If a story was written largely by AI, say so when you send it. It is not a secret, and hiding it is a breach of trust.

The bottom line: if the story contains a claim you cannot defend in a correction, do not publish it. A writer who checks is worth more than a writer who is fast.

Before you publish anything AI helped with

  • Every fact checked against a source outside the AI tool.
  • No fabricated quotes, sources or statistics.
  • The opening paragraph written or rewritten by you, not copied from the headline.
  • The story reads in our voice and follows the same review checks as any other.

10. Getting featured: author of the week

Publishing consistently can also put your name and picture at the top of our editorial team page.

  • Every week the editorial team page names an Author of the Week: the journalist who published the most stories since the last Sunday.
  • The count is reset every Sunday morning. A Sunday always starts a fresh week, so at the very start of a week only that day's stories have been counted so far.
  • It is decided by stories published, not by views or likes. A steady habit of putting out good, verified work is what earns the slot.
  • If nobody publishes in a given week, the slot stays open and shows "Yet to decide" rather than a name.
  • When you are the author of the week, your picture, your bio and your latest story are shown at the top of the page, and every reader who opens it can tap through to your portfolio.

One deadline that matters: a story only counts once it is actually published. A draft still in the editor does not count toward the week.

Questions about a rule? Ask the desk before you publish, not after.
